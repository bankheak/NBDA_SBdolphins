### Abstract
Human food subsidies are rapidly reshaping wildlife behavior, yet the mechanisms governing the spread of novel human-associated foraging behaviors remain poorly understood. Using a long-term individual-based study of wild bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) in Sarasota Bay, Florida, we examined the social transmission and diffusion of two human-subsidized foraging behaviors: exploiting hook-and-line and fixed-gear fisheries. Using Bayesian Multi-Network-Based Diffusion Analysis (NBDA), we demonstrate strong evidence for social transmission via both horizontal and vertical learning pathways. Crucially, fixed-gear foraging showed stronger evidence of social transmission across both peer-to-peer and mother-offspring social networks than hook-and-line foraging. To isolate the sources of this difference, we quantified the spatiotemporal landscape of both human-subsidies using Bayesian generalized additive models. While recreational hook-and-line fishing subsidies were sparse and variable across space and time, fixed-gear fishing subsidies provided highly abundant, spatially aggregated, and seasonally predictable food resources, which directly correlated with its accelerated and more consistent social transmission. These findings reveal that resource predictability can shape not only foraging innovation but also be a major catalyst of social transmission within wildlife populations. Consequently, predicting and managing human–wildlife interactions and conflict require integrating the dynamics of both food resources and wildlife social dynamics.
