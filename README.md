### Abstract
Human subsidies have rapidly reshaped the processes in which social animals make foraging decisions by creating novel food resources. Bottlenose dolphins (Tursiops truncatus) in Sarasota Bay, Florida, provide a rare opportunity to examine how human-centric foraging behaviors originate and spread, owing to more than five decades of individual-based monitoring of life histories, social relationships, and foraging behavior. Following severe harmful algal blooms in the mid-2000s that reduced natural prey availability, human-centric foraging behaviors increased throughout the population. Using Network-Based Diffusion Analysis (NBDA) and multilayer social networks, we investigated the transmission pathways of two classes of human-centric foraging behavior: boat-based and fixed-gear foraging. We found strong evidence for social transmission through both peer-to-peer and mother-offspring pathways, with fixed-gear foraging spreading substantially faster through the social network than boat-based behaviors. To explain these differences, we quantified the abundance and predictability of associated human-derived food resources using Bayesian generalized additive models. Fixed fishing gear was more abundant and exhibited predictable spatial and seasonal aggregation, whereas recreational fishing-line resources were less abundant and more variable across space and time. This greater predictability was associated with faster and more consistent social transmission. Together, these results demonstrate that resource predictability can shape not only whether novel foraging behaviors spread, but also how quickly they diffuse through socially connected wildlife populations. Our findings highlight the importance of considering both resource dynamics and social networks when predicting and managing human–wildlife interactions.
